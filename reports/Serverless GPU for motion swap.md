# Serverless saves minutes, not money, for motion swap

RunPod Serverless would let anyone on the team press Render without someone first starting a pod, and the code change is modest (about 450 new lines and 100 changed, with the three graph builders, the workflow JSONs, the front end and `comfy_client.py` untouched), but at the team's volumes it does not save money: a 10 s Standard Wan Animate 2 clip costs about **$0.37 on a serverless RTX PRO 4500 or $0.57 on a serverless L40S, against $0.23 of render time on the A40 pod the earlier cost report recommends**, and a month of 100 such clips lands at roughly **$45 (PRO 4500) or $64 (L40S) serverless versus $40 on the A40 pod and $54 on the L40S pod**, all before the cost of the always-on box the FastAPI app would need once there is no pod to live on. Serverless wins only for sessions of three or fewer clips, and even then by cents per clip; what it buys is operational, not financial: no forgotten pod at $14 to $24 a day, no 45-minute wait for someone to boot a GPU, and a bill that tracks clips rather than hours. The mechanics work for our workload because the existing network volume mounts inside serverless workers at `/runpod-volume`, so models, inputs and outputs stay where they are and the 10 MB request limit never sees a driving video; the real risks are per-job cold starts of two to four billed minutes, a 10-minute default timeout that would kill every High or Finish render until raised, a single-datacentre GPU pool, and jobs that silently sit in the queue with no shell to look into. Among platforms, RunPod fits because the volume is already there; Modal is the only credible second (24-hour timeouts, 2.5 GB/s volumes, $30 of monthly credits) and Vast.ai the only one with a vendor-maintained ComfyUI video template; Replicate, Koyeb, Beam, Baseten, Cloud Run and SageMaker each fail on timeout, storage or price. The recommendation is **not now**: finish the pending Phase 3 GPU session, instrument the Costs page to count session sizes for four to six weeks, and build the worker (four to five working days, $5 to $10 of GPU) only if the team's real pattern turns out to be scattered single clips, a cheap always-on app host is confirmed, and the realised pod cost per output second stays above the $0.04 hosted floor. Every figure is tagged [M] measured in this project, [P] published by a vendor, [C] community anecdote, or [I] inferred by arithmetic from those.

## A worker is a GPU that exists only while your clip renders

Today the team rents one GPU computer (a pod) by the hour. Someone starts it, waits several minutes while ComfyUI boots and the models load, the team renders clips through the app that runs on the same machine, and a watchdog stops the pod 20 minutes after the last job. The meter runs the whole time, whether a clip is rendering or not, which is why the earlier cost report found that idle and setup, not rendering, make a realistic month 1.4 to 1.7 times the pure render cost ([Motion swap engine running costs](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/reports/Motion%20swap%20engine%20running%20costs.md)). Serverless changes the unit of rental from an hour of a machine to a job on a machine. You publish a recipe (a Docker image) that says how to build a computer able to run our ComfyUI graphs, and you give RunPod an address (an endpoint) where jobs are dropped. A **worker** is one such computer, built from the recipe, that RunPod starts when a job arrives and tears down when there is nothing left to do. RunPod describes workers as "container instances that run your custom Docker image" whose lifecycle it manages automatically ([RunPod Serverless overview](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/overview)).

Here is what would happen between pressing Render and getting the clip, step by step, with the meter state at each stage. First, the browser uploads the reference image and driving video to the app exactly as now, and the app copies them onto the shared network volume as `app_<jobid>_image.png` and `app_<jobid>_video.mp4`, which is what `stage_input()` already does ([app/jobs.py L292-298](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)). The meter is off; nothing has a GPU yet. Second, the app sends a small message (a few tens of kilobytes: the 29 to 57 node graph plus the job id and seed) to the endpoint's `/run` address, and RunPod answers with a job id and the state `IN_QUEUE`, meaning "waiting in the endpoint queue for an available worker" ([RunPod job states](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/job-states)). The meter is still off; queue time is never billed ([RunPod billing blog, Oct 2026](https://www.runpod.io/blog/failed-requests-real-charges-how-runpod-serverless-billing-works-when-workers-fail)). Third, RunPod looks for a free GPU of the kind we asked for, in the datacentre where our volume lives, and if no worker is running it starts one: it copies our image onto that host (not billed if it happens before the worker starts, which is the normal case once a host has seen the image) and then starts the container, at which point **the meter starts** ([RunPod Serverless pricing](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/pricing)). Fourth, inside the worker ComfyUI boots and the first graph makes it read the 24 GB of Wan Animate 2 weights (or about 40 GB for SCAIL-2 with its shared encoders) off the network volume into the GPU; this is the cold start, it is billed, and RunPod's own guidance is that end-to-end cold starts "typically run 30 seconds to two minutes plus depending on image size and model" ([RunPod FlashBoot blog, Jul 2026](https://www.runpod.io/blog/serverless-gpu-cold-starts-flashboot)). The job state becomes `IN_PROGRESS`. Fifth, the render runs for the same 6 to 35 minutes it takes on a pod today, billed per second. The worker writes the result MP4 into `motion_swap/<jobid>/s<seed>_result.mp4` on the volume, exactly where ComfyUI writes it on the pod. Sixth, the worker's handler returns a short list of output file names (not the video itself) and the job becomes `COMPLETED`. Seventh, the worker waits for the idle timeout (5 seconds by default, longer if we set it) in case another job arrives, **billed while it waits**, then stops, and **the meter stops** ([RunPod endpoint configurations](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/endpoint-configurations)). Eighth, the app, which has been polling the job's `/status` every couple of seconds just as it polls ComfyUI's `/history` today, sees `COMPLETED`, reads the result off the volume, builds the side-by-side compare clip and the contact sheets on its own CPU as it does now, and the card in the browser flips to done.

Two consequences of this choreography matter for a non-engineer. The bill for a job is "from when a worker starts until it fully stops, rounded up to the nearest second", which includes the cold start, the render and the idle wait but excludes queueing, image download and the time the worker is scaled down ([RunPod Serverless pricing](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/pricing)). And a job that fails late is billed for all the time it ran: RunPod's own example is that "a failed job that ran 9 minutes bills 9 minutes", and a job that hits the execution timeout bills right up to the timeout ([RunPod billing blog, Oct 2026](https://www.runpod.io/blog/failed-requests-real-charges-how-runpod-serverless-billing-works-when-workers-fail)). There is one more state worth knowing: RunPod's FlashBoot keeps a finished worker paused with its models still in GPU memory for an unpublished grace period, so a second job arriving soon after the first resumes in "about 200ms" and skips the cold start entirely; but a paused worker "can be evicted if GPU capacity is constrained by another workload", after which the next job pays the full cold start again ([RunPod FlashBoot blog](https://www.runpod.io/blog/serverless-gpu-cold-starts-flashboot)). For a team that fires a few renders an hour, the honest planning assumption is that most jobs start cold.

The app itself also needs a home. On a pod it runs beside ComfyUI; with serverless there is no pod, so the FastAPI process must run somewhere that is always on and can see the volume's `input/` and `output/` folders. The cheapest option that needs no new storage code is a small CPU pod on RunPod with the same volume mounted at `/workspace`; whether CPU pods can attach network volumes, and what they cost per hour, was not verified in this research and must be checked in the console before anything is built ([our_pipeline_design.md, KQ3](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/research_notes/Serverless%20GPU%20for%20motion%20swap/our_pipeline_design.md)). The arithmetic to keep in mind is that an always-on host costs 720 times its hourly rate per month, so every cent per hour is $7.20 a month [I].

## The code already has a seven-function seam, so the change is about 550 lines

The app is one FastAPI process that owns a sequential `Runner` thread; the Runner builds an API-format ComfyUI graph per seed, POSTs it to ComfyUI over HTTP, polls `/history`, then reads the rendered files straight off the local disk to build compare clips and contact sheets ([app/jobs.py L697-846](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)). Everything that talks to ComfyUI goes through seven functions in one file, `tools/comfy_client.py`: `queue_prompt`, `poll`, `queue_contains`, `collect_outputs`, `error_message`, the `ComfyError` type and the pure graph helper `set_input` ([tools/comfy_client.py L82-132](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/tools/comfy_client.py)). The Runner calls exactly four of them from `adopt`, `wait`, `queue_run` and `render` ([app/jobs.py L731-787](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)). That is the whole network seam. Abstracting it means wrapping four calls behind a `Backend` interface, not restructuring the Runner.

The filesystem seam is wider but equally concentrated. `stage_input`, `pad_reference` and `fit_view` write into ComfyUI's input directory; `make_compare` and `make_sheets` read outputs and write derived files; `save_job`, `load_job` and `list_jobs` keep job records under `ComfyUI/output/_jobs`; `server.py` serves outputs from local disk and checks `p.exists()` on every path ([app/jobs.py L292-319, L449-565, L570-606](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py), [app/web/server.py L159-169, L218-236, L491-516](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/web/server.py)). All of it goes through two module constants, `COMFY_INPUT` and `COMFY_OUTPUT`, which the tests already override ([tests/test_app.py L153-154](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/tests/test_app.py)). This is the fact that makes the minimal design possible: RunPod mounts the same network volume at `/workspace` in pods and at `/runpod-volume` in serverless workers, and the volume exists "to share data across multiple machines and Runpod products" ([RunPod network volumes](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/storage/network-volumes)). Our persistent tree is `/workspace/creator-data/{models,input,output,user,venv}` ([scripts/setup.sh L17-20](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/scripts/setup.sh)); the worker sees it as `/runpod-volume/creator-data/...`. Relative output paths such as `motion_swap/<jobid>/s1_result.mp4` are identical on both sides even though the mount points differ, so `collect_outputs` returns strings the app can resolve unchanged. Pointing the two constants at the volume keeps every filesystem-dependent function working as is.

Without a shared volume none of this holds. RunPod's `/run` accepts 10 MB and `/runsync` 20 MB per request, and for larger results the docs tell you to store them in cloud storage and return links ([RunPod handler functions](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/workers/handler-functions)). The app accepts uploads up to 600 MB ([app/web/server.py L32](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/web/server.py)); a 50 MB driving video base64-encoded is about 67 MB, over both limits [I]. A laptop-hosted or Fly.io-hosted app would therefore need an S3 bucket for inputs and outputs and roughly 200 more lines of storage code. The minimal design avoids that by keeping the app on RunPod next to the volume, which is also why the app host question above is load-bearing.

The worker image is simpler than the usual ComfyUI serverless build because the project depends on no third-party node packs: every `class_type` in `workflows/*.json` except the four `Creator_*` nodes ships in the pinned ComfyUI commit's `comfy_extras` (`nodes_wan.py`, `nodes_scail.py`, `nodes_seedvr.py`, `nodes_sam3.py`, `nodes_frame_interpolation.py`, `nodes_video.py`), and `creator_nodes` imports only numpy, torch and PIL ([creator_nodes/loader.py L4-6](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/creator_nodes/loader.py)). The stock `runpod/worker-comfyui` image cannot be used directly for three reasons: it only scans the `images` key of node outputs and ignores video, which an open issue from August 2026 confirms makes video workflows "unusable through the API" without modification ([worker-comfyui issue #232](https://github.com/runpod-workers/worker-comfyui/issues/232)); it pins ComfyUI by release version rather than our commit `e8665ca8` ([versions.env](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/versions.env)); and it expects models at `/runpod-volume/models` rather than under `creator-data`. Its pattern, however, is exactly right and is what our handler copies: start ComfyUI inside the container, wait for it, queue the prompt, wait for history, report outputs ([worker-comfyui handler.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/runpod-workers/worker-comfyui/main/handler.py)). Our handler is about 120 lines that reuse `queue_prompt`, `poll`, `collect_outputs` and `error_message` verbatim, poll `/history` every 2 s with `runpod.serverless.progress_update` every 30 s so the card can show elapsed time, write a durable `s<seed>_worker.json` to the volume (because RunPod keeps `/run` results for only 30 minutes after completion, which matters if the app restarts mid-render ([RunPod send requests](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/send-requests))), and return the output list. ComfyUI is started with `--models-directory`, `--input-directory`, `--output-directory` and `--user-directory` flags pointing under `$CREATOR_DATA`, which the pinned checkout supports ([ComfyUI/comfy/cli_args.py L70-74](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/ComfyUI/comfy/cli_args.py)); flags rather than `extra_model_paths.yaml` because only flags redirect input and output too.

On the app side, a new `app/backends.py` holds `PodBackend` (pure delegation to today's four calls) and `EndpointBackend`, whose `submit` is `POST /run` with `{"input": {"workflow", "extra_data", "job_id", "seed"}, "policy": {"executionTimeout": ...}}`, whose `poll` is `GET /status/{id}` translating `IN_QUEUE`/`IN_PROGRESS`/`COMPLETED`/`FAILED`/`TIMED_OUT`/`CANCELLED` into the entry-like dict the Runner already understands, and whose `lost` is a 404 after the job's TTL ([RunPod operation reference](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/operation-reference)). `Runner.__init__` picks the backend from `RENDER_BACKEND=pod|endpoint`; `queue_run` passes an `executionTimeout` of at least 1,200 s and twice the per-seed estimate, because the endpoint default of 600 s would kill most of our renders ([RunPod send requests](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/send-requests)); `render` records `executionTime` from the status response so cold start shows separately from render time. Seedance jobs are API-node calls that need no GPU ([app/jobs.py L32, L775-779](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)) and must never go to the GPU endpoint, where they would bill about seven minutes of idle GPU per clip; they route to a `--cpu` ComfyUI beside the app, a profile that already exists in `.claude/launch.json`. `cost_summary` learns to add `executionTime x GPU_HOURLY_USD / 3600` for endpoint jobs and to skip the pod-hours estimate for them, and `status_info` shows the endpoint's `/health` worker counts instead of "stops in N min".

## Per clip: $0.37 to $0.57 serverless against $0.23 of A40 render time

The serverless rates are RunPod's listed per-hour Flex prices divided by 3,600: the 48 GB L40S tier at $1.75 per hour is $0.000486 per second, the 32 GB RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell at $1.15 is $0.000319, and the 48 GB A40/A6000 tier at $1.22 is $0.000339 ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)) [P, per-second figures I]. The pod baselines are the earlier cost report's: A40 at the $0.60 per hour the project paid, L40S at the $1.09 list price, A100 at the $1.79 paid ([Motion swap engine running costs](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/reports/Motion%20swap%20engine%20running%20costs.md)). Render times are the project's own: a Standard 81-frame pass took 464 to 469 s on the A40 [M], about 360 s on the A100 [M] and 359 s averaged over two seeds on an RTX PRO 4500 [M]; a High pass about 560 s on the A100 [M]; the "post it" deliverable (High, two passes, Finish) 2,057 s on the A100 [M] ([docs/TEST_LOG.md](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/TEST_LOG.md)). L40S time is set equal to A100 time because on Wan 2.2 A14B the two cards were within 1% of each other in an independent benchmark ([SaladCloud](https://blog.salad.com/benchmarking-wan2-2/)) [I]. A 10 s clip is three chained passes, the app's `MAX_CHUNKS` [M].

The cold-start term is the one number nobody has measured for this workload. RunPod says network volumes deliver "200-400 MB/s" ([RunPod network volumes](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/storage/network-volumes)) [P], so the 24 GB Wan Animate 2 profile is 60 to 120 s of pure reading and the roughly 40 GB SCAIL-2 set is 100 to 200 s [I]; a vLLM post measured 32 GB read from a volume in "about 21 seconds" with prefetching, about 1.5 GB/s, which shows the spec range is conservative on a good day ([RunPod vLLM cold start blog, Aug 2026](https://www.runpod.io/blog/cut-vllm-cold-starts-runpod-serverless)) [P]. ComfyUI's own boot added about 43 s in one practitioner's serverless setup ([answeroverflow Discord excerpt, Nov 2025](https://www.answeroverflow.com/m/1443232561236017193)) [C]. The project's best anchor is its own pod: "model load adds 1 to 2 minutes to the first clip of a session" reading the same volume ([docs/TEST_LOG.md L25](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/TEST_LOG.md)) [M]. The one Wan 14B serverless worker author found budgeted "+1 to 3 min" for 38 GB of weights and labelled it unmeasured ([fakinflu-video-worker README, Oct 2026](https://github.com/romingodelfuego/fakinflu-video-worker)) [C]. The table therefore uses 90 s of cold start plus the 5 s default idle, with 150 s and a 300 s worst case (an uncached image pull inside the billed window) shown as sensitivities. Because ComfyUI loads weights lazily on the first graph, most of that time lands inside the job's `executionTime` rather than its `delayTime`; the bill is the same either way, but the execution timeout must leave room for it [I].

Three readings. First, **the RTX PRO 4500 is the serverless card for Standard work**: at $0.37 per 10 s clip it undercuts the serverless L40S by a third and even the serverless A40 tier, because the project measured it at A100 speed for two thirds of the A40 tier's price; the earlier report reached the same conclusion [I from M]. It is a 32 GB card, which is the floor the presets require for High tier ([app/presets.yaml](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/presets.yaml)), so High and Finish should carry a 48 GB fallback in the endpoint's GPU priority list. Second, the cold start is a small share of the money: 90 to 150 s on an 18-minute render is 8 to 14% of the bill, about $0.03 to $0.07 per clip at PRO 4500 rates [I]; its cost is mostly waiting time and risk, covered below. Third, a two-seed job, which the team has used on every logged render, pays the cold start twice if the worker scales down in the gap between seeds. With the default 5 s idle timeout it will: the Runner spends longer than that collecting outputs and building the compare clip before queueing the next seed ([app/jobs.py L789-846](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)). Setting the idle timeout to about 120 to 180 s costs $0.04 to $0.06 per job at PRO 4500 rates and $0.06 to $0.09 on the L40S, roughly the price of one cold start, so it is a wash for single seeds and a win for multiple seeds [I]. Running seeds in parallel on separate workers (max workers defaults to 3) is the better fix and a 40-line follow-up.

The monthly picture reuses the earlier report's scenarios: a 10 s Standard clip, one seed, no Finish; pods carry 30% of render time as in-session review gaps plus 25 minutes per session of boot, model load and the 20-minute watchdog allowance, plus the $7 per month for the 100 GB volume at $0.07 per GB-month ([RunPod Serverless pricing](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/pricing)) [P]; serverless carries the same $7 and 90 s of cold start on every clip but no session or gap cost ([Motion swap engine running costs](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/reports/Motion%20swap%20engine%20running%20costs.md)). The break-even is the same one the earlier report derived: with an L40S pod clip at $0.33 of render plus $0.10 of gaps and $0.45 of fixed per-session cost, and a serverless L40S clip at $0.57, sessions of three or fewer clips are cheaper serverless; against the A40 pod ($0.30 variable, $0.25 per session) with a serverless PRO 4500 at $0.37 the break-even is about 3.3 clips per session [I]. A single always-on Active worker is not an escape: RunPod now prices Active workers "through sales inquiry" ([RunPod Serverless pricing](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/pricing)), its 2024 blog table put the discount at about 20 to 30% below Flex ([RunPod price-cut blog](https://www.runpod.io/blog/runpod-slashes-gpu-prices-more-power-less-cost-for-ai-builders)) [P, dated], and an L40S Active worker for an 8-hour workday at $1.14 to $1.40 an hour is $200 to $245 a month against $192 for the pod over the same hours, so it is a pod with worse pricing [I].

Where the money does move is the worst case the serverless model removes. The runbook prices a forgotten pod at $14 to $24 a day for the A40 to L40S range ([docs/MOTION_SWAP_RUNBOOK.md](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/MOTION_SWAP_RUNBOOK.md)) [M-based], and the watchdog exists to cap it; a scale-to-zero endpoint cannot be forgotten. The symmetric serverless failure is a long idle timeout, which a third-party guide warns "can make a scale-to-zero endpoint quietly bill like an always-on one" ([theneuralbase](https://theneuralbase.com/runpod/learn/beginner/billing-surprises/)) [C], and a job that hangs until a generous execution timeout, which bills to the timeout ([RunPod billing blog](https://www.runpod.io/blog/failed-requests-real-charges-how-runpod-serverless-billing-works-when-workers-fail)) [P]. With a 3,600 s timeout and a PRO 4500 that is $1.15 per stuck job [I], a bounded and visible loss rather than a day's bill.

## Six risks, of which cold starts and timeouts are certain and GPU scarcity is the one that hurts

Cold start is a certainty, not a risk, and its size is the one number this project must measure before deciding anything. The inferred two to four billed minutes per fresh worker (ComfyUI boot plus 24 to 40 GB off the volume) sits inside RunPod's "30 seconds to two minutes plus" guidance for a large model, but no source anywhere measures a ComfyUI 14B video cold start on RunPod Serverless with a timing breakdown ([runpod_serverless_mechanics.md, KQ2 gaps](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/research_notes/Serverless%20GPU%20for%20motion%20swap/runpod_serverless_mechanics.md)). Two mechanisms could make it worse than the arithmetic. ComfyUI loads safetensors through memory mapping, and a long-open ComfyUI issue measured that path at "about a 30-50x slowdown" on network-backed storage, an SDXL file taking 40 to 50 s instead of 1 to 2 s ([Comfy-Org/ComfyUI issue #2288](https://github.com/Comfy-Org/ComfyUI/issues/2288)) [C]; the project's own 1 to 2 minute load from the same volume on a pod suggests the effect is tolerable here, but a serverless host may sit on a different network path than the pods did. And RunPod marks a worker unhealthy if the cold start exceeds 7 minutes unless `RUNPOD_INIT_TIMEOUT` is raised ([RunPod optimization guide](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/development/optimization)) [P]; because our handler is "ready" as soon as ComfyUI answers `/system_stats`, before any model loads, the cutoff only threatens the ComfyUI boot itself, but the variable should be set to 800 s regardless. The levers, in order of value, are a longer idle timeout so consecutive seeds share a worker, FlashBoot (on by default) for jobs that arrive within minutes of each other, and listing the PRO 4500 first so the load is cheap when it happens.

Timeouts are the second certainty. The endpoint's execution timeout defaults to 600 s, and the request-level `executionTimeout` "applies only while a worker is actively running the job" and can be set from 5 s to 7 days ([RunPod send requests](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/endpoints/send-requests)) [P]. Our renders are 6 minutes for one Standard pass, 18 for a 10 s Standard clip, 28 for 10 s High, and 34 minutes measured for the "post it" deliverable [M]; the default would kill all but the first. RunPod's own benchmark of Wan 2.2 at 720p, 81 frames and 20 steps shows why this is routine for this model class: 1,520 s on an L40S, 2,654 s on an RTX A6000, 2,749 s on an A40 ([RunPod best GPU for AI video, Oct 2026](https://www.runpod.io/articles/guides/best-gpu-for-ai-video)) [P]. The fix is mechanical (endpoint default 3,600 s, per-request value at twice the estimate with a 1,200 s floor) and RunPod's advice is to set it a little above the real p99 and "never turn it off in production" ([RunPod billing blog](https://www.runpod.io/blog/failed-requests-real-charges-how-runpod-serverless-billing-works-when-workers-fail)) [P]. The residual risk is a worker that loses its host mid-render: no serverless Wan worker found in this research checkpoints partial progress, so a 30-minute job that dies at minute 29 is rendered again from scratch and billed twice [I].

Payload limits are solved by the shared volume and only become a risk if the design drifts. The 10 MB and 20 MB request limits ([RunPod handler functions](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/workers/handler-functions)) [P] never see a video because inputs are already on the volume and outputs are returned as file names. The two rules that keep this safe are that exactly one app instance writes `job.json` (RunPod warns that "writing to the same volume from multiple workers simultaneously may cause data corruption" ([RunPod network volumes](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/storage/network-volumes)) [P], and our per-seed output files are already disjoint), and that the volume is never allowed to lapse: at a $0 account balance a network volume "may eventually be terminated and its data cannot be recovered" [P], and Global Volumes are deleted 15 days after the balance hits zero ([RunPod Global Volumes blog, Oct 2026](https://www.runpod.io/blog/global-volumes-serverless-beta)) [P].

GPU availability is the risk that can actually block the team, and it is structural. Attaching a network volume "constrains worker deployments to that volume's datacenter, which may limit GPU availability and reduce failover options" ([RunPod network volumes](https://docs.runpod.io/serverless/storage/network-volumes)) [P]. Our 60 to 65 GB of models already live in one datacentre; if that datacentre does not carry the PRO 4500 or has its 48 GB hosts delisted, jobs queue and nothing in our code can move them. This is not hypothetical for this model class: the author of a Wan Animate 2 serverless worker had an automated GPU test "fail because no 48 GB GPU was free" in September 2026 ([PrinceMargaret/wan2.2-animate-2 PR #2](https://github.com/PrinceMargaret/wan2.2-animate-2/pull/2)) [C], and a Discord thread from October 2025 describes an endpoint throttled for days because RunPod had delisted the hosts for maintenance ([answeroverflow Discord excerpt](https://www.answeroverflow.com/m/1427672244511047863)) [C]. Mitigations are to list up to three GPU types in priority order (PRO 4500, then L40S, then A40), to pick the datacentre for the volume by GPU inventory rather than inheriting it, and to keep the pod path alive as the fallback; RunPod's Global Volumes beta removes the datacentre pin but is object-store backed, publishes no throughput, and cannot be combined with a network volume ([RunPod Global Volumes blog](https://www.runpod.io/blog/global-volumes-serverless-beta)) [P]. The pod has the same single-datacentre exposure today, so serverless does not add this risk so much as remove the human who notices and picks another GPU.

Stuck jobs are the failure mode practitioners report most, and they are silent. An endpoint can look healthy while jobs sit `IN_QUEUE` forever because workers start, crash before the handler runs, and restart; one write-up traces this to a template variable changed without the matching code pin, with the fix being to read a worker's log rather than the endpoint status ([dev.to: RunPod serverless jobs stuck in queue](https://dev.to/ownstackhq/runpod-serverless-jobs-stuck-in-queue-forever-after-changing-modelid-47g5)) [C]. Two Discord threads describe idle workers that stopped consuming jobs for minutes, where "the only reliable solution" was scaling the endpoint to zero and back ([answeroverflow excerpts, Jan 2024 and Apr 2025](https://www.answeroverflow.com/m/1367215470889144371)) [C]. The official worker-comfyui had a hardcoded 25 s wait for ComfyUI that made the first job after a cold start fail whenever large checkpoints loaded slowly, fixed only in March 2026 ([worker-comfyui issue #186](https://github.com/runpod-workers/worker-comfyui/issues/186)) [C]; our handler must poll as long as the ComfyUI process is alive, as the fix does. The cheapest insurance is a client-side rule in the Runner: if a job stays `IN_QUEUE` longer than the expected cold start plus a margin, surface it on the card and offer a one-click endpoint reset.

Debugging is the risk that costs engineering time rather than money. There is no `tail -f logs/comfy.log` and no shell; what you get is RunPod's per-worker log stream, the `/status` output and whatever the handler writes to the volume. The mitigations are already in the design: the same image runs as a pod template with `SERVE_API_LOCALLY=true`, which exposes the handler as a local HTTP API you can hit from a shell on the pod, as worker-comfyui does ([worker-comfyui start.sh](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/runpod-workers/worker-comfyui/main/src/start.sh)); `COMFY_LOG_LEVEL=INFO`; optional SSH into a worker via a `PUBLIC_KEY` variable; `tools/validate_workflow.py` run against the container before any endpoint deploy; and the `s<seed>_worker.json` the handler leaves on the volume as a durable record. Image drift is the slow version of the same risk: the image pins ComfyUI by commit but torch and CUDA only by index URL, so every ComfyUI update becomes a rebuild, push and smoke test rather than a `git fetch` on the pod.

## RunPod fits because the volume is already there; Modal is the only real alternative

The platform question is mostly answered by one fact: the 60 to 65 GB of models and the team's inputs and outputs already sit on a RunPod network volume that serverless workers can mount, and no other vendor can see it. Moving platforms means re-downloading the models into that vendor's storage, rewriting the worker in that vendor's SDK, and solving input and output transfer with S3 because the app can no longer share a filesystem with the worker. ## Decision: not now; build when three conditions hold, in three steps

The evidence supports building the serverless path later and not now, for three reasons that stack. The dollar case is weak: at every realistic volume the saving against the A40 pod is single-digit dollars a month, it turns negative once an app host is priced in, and at batch usage the pod is $5 to $20 a month cheaper [I]. The engineering case is real but small: four to five working days for the minimal design, plus an ongoing maintenance cost (image rebuilds on every ComfyUI update, a second runbook, a new class of silent failure) on a two-engine app whose Phase 3 verification of SCAIL-2 has not yet happened ([docs/ROADMAP.md L100](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/ROADMAP.md)). And the decisive numbers are unmeasured: nobody knows the team's real session-size distribution, the cold start for our image on a serverless host, or whether a CPU pod can mount the volume and at what price. Building before those are known is building on the earlier report's $0.57 estimate, which itself carries a 90 s cold-start guess.

The conditions under which the answer flips to "build" are concrete. First, usage: after four to six weeks of normal work the Costs page shows that at least half of GPU sessions hold three or fewer clips, or the team reports being blocked because nobody wanted to start a pod for one clip. The earlier report gave the same signal in a different unit: if the realised pod cost per output second stays above the $0.04 per second hosted Wan2.2-Animate rate for a few months, "the pod is being started too often for too little, and that is the signal to build the serverless worker" ([Motion swap engine running costs](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/reports/Motion%20swap%20engine%20running%20costs.md)). Second, hosting: a RunPod CPU pod (or equivalent) that can mount the existing volume is confirmed in the console at a monthly cost below the expected saving, which at team volume means well under $15 a month, or the team accepts that the host is paid for the operational benefit rather than the saving. Third, inventory: the volume's datacentre is confirmed to carry the RTX PRO 4500 and a 48 GB fallback for serverless, or the team is willing to create a second volume elsewhere and re-download 60 to 65 GB (about 15 minutes on a pod per the runbook ([docs/MOTION_SWAP_RUNBOOK.md](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/MOTION_SWAP_RUNBOOK.md)) [M]). If the first condition never materialises, the right answer is "not at all" and the roadmap's existing note stands ([docs/ROADMAP.md L121](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/docs/ROADMAP.md)).

Step 0 costs nothing and should happen now. Run the pending Phase 3 session on an A40 (or an L40S if speed matters), time SCAIL-2 and the L40S per pass so the earlier report's [I] tags become [M], and set `GPU_HOURLY_USD` to the real price before the first job so the Costs page is right. Add to `cost_summary` a count of clips per session and the realised dollars per output second, both derivable from the per-day spans and `run["seconds"]` it already computes ([app/jobs.py L634-683](C:/Users/user/Desktop/creator-pipeline/app/jobs.py)), and show them on the Costs tab. Check two things in the RunPod console while the pod is up: which GPUs the volume's datacentre offers for serverless, and whether a CPU pod can attach the volume and at what hourly rate. That is roughly half a day of work and the GPU time is the Phase 3 session the project already plans to buy.

Step 1, if the conditions hold, is the image proven on a pod: about one day of work and one to two hours of GPU, $0.60 to $2.20 on an A40 or L40S pod [I from P]. Write `worker/Dockerfile`, `worker/start.sh`, `worker/handler.py` and `worker/test_input.json`; build locally (Docker Desktop on the laptop; expect about 10 GB because of torch) and push to a registry; deploy it as a pod template on the existing volume via `tools/pod.py` with `SERVE_API_LOCALLY=true` and `CREATOR_DATA=/workspace/creator-data`; hit the SDK's local `/run` with the smoke input; run `tools/validate_workflow.py` against the container's ComfyUI for every graph in `workflows/`; and read ComfyUI import time and model load time off the logs. The exit criterion is one Wan Standard pass and one SCAIL-2 pass rendered through the handler into `/workspace/creator-data/output/`. This step has standalone value even if step 2 never happens: a pinned, reproducible image replaces the per-pod `pip install` and makes `docs/UPDATING.md` a build rather than a procedure.

Step 2 is the endpoint and the measurement that decides everything: about half a day of work and about one hour of billed GPU, $3 to $5 including several deliberate cold starts at $1.15 to $1.75 an hour [I from P]. Create the endpoint in the volume's datacentre with the step 1 image, the volume attached, GPU priority RTX PRO 4500 then L40S then A40, execution timeout 3,600 s, idle timeout 180 s, active workers 0, max workers 2, FlashBoot on, `RUNPOD_INIT_TIMEOUT=800`, `COMFY_LOG_LEVEL=INFO`. Send the smoke job with curl, poll `/status`, record `delayTime` and `executionTime`; send it again immediately to measure the warm case; wait ten minutes and send it again to see whether FlashBoot survives a realistic gap; send a graph that fails on purpose to see the `FAILED` shape; and write every number into `docs/TEST_LOG.md` and the serverless column of `docs/COSTS.md`. If the measured cold start is above about four minutes or the warm case does not survive a ten-minute gap, the per-clip figures in this report move up by $0.05 to $0.10 and the case for step 3 weakens further.

Step 3 is the app switch: 1.5 to 2 days of work and no GPU beyond the app host. Implement `app/backends.py` and the Runner changes, `CREATOR_DATA` paths, Seedance routing to the CPU ComfyUI, `status_info` health, cost accounting from `executionTime`, the tests, `start.sh app` and `setup.sh --app-only`, and the runbook. Stand up the app host next to the volume, run the whole flow from the browser (upload, preflight, job, endpoint render, compare and sheets built by the app, clips served from `/files/output/`), and keep `RENDER_BACKEND=pod` as the default until a week of mixed use shows the Costs tab favouring the endpoint for the team's actual session sizes. The follow-ups that are deliberately outside the minimal design are parallel seeds across workers (about 40 lines in `render`, the biggest lever on multi-seed latency), S3-backed inputs and outputs so the app can run off RunPod, a webhook in place of polling, and a per-request GPU tier so SCAIL-2 Standard goes to the 32 GB card and High and Finish to 48 GB.

## Conclusion

The research reframes serverless from a cost tool into a convenience tool. The earlier cost report already showed that idle and session overhead, not GPU choice, drive the monthly bill; this report shows that serverless attacks exactly that overhead and still cannot save more than a few dollars a month at the team's volumes, because Flex pricing is 1.5 to 2.4 times the pod rate and every clip pays two to four minutes of cold start, while the pod's overhead is already capped by the watchdog. What serverless changes is who can render and when: anyone, any time, with no pod to start or forget. That is worth something to a small team, but it is worth paying for only once the team knows how often it is blocked by the pod, and the project does not yet have that number.

Two things learned here are useful regardless of the decision. The RTX PRO 4500 is the best card the project has measured for Standard work on any billing model, and the pod table's absence of that card means the A40 remains the pod choice while the PRO 4500 would be the serverless choice. And the ComfyUI seam the app already has (four calls in the Runner, two path constants everywhere else) is cleaner than the typical ComfyUI deployment, which is why the whole migration is about 550 lines rather than a rewrite; keeping that seam clean as SCAIL-2 and the Finish pass evolve is the cheapest way to keep the serverless option open without building it.
